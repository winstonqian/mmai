Final Project

NeuroCLIP: Action Semantics Governs EEG Concept Decodability

Winston Qian  ·  Rachel Li  ·  Emma Wang  ·  MIT · Spring 2026

Thesis: In contrastive EEG-to-CLIP retrieval, concept decodability is not determined by CLIP embedding geometry — it is determined by action semantic content. Concepts depicting dynamic human activity are decoded with ~80% higher accuracy than passive object scenes (6.8% vs. 3.8%, p < 10⁻⁷), consistently across all subjects and recording sessions. This is not a raw EEG amplitude effect — it is a dataset-agnostic principle for designing more decodable BCI stimulus sets.

Phase 1 — Baseline Audit First, We Made Sure the Baseline Was Real

Before building anything new, we had a nagging question: does EEG2Video actually decode visual perception, or is it decoding experimental protocol artifacts? SEED-DV presents concepts in structured 5-clip runs — a cue followed by five consecutive clips of the same concept. That creates a real risk that models learn run-position patterns (anticipation, adaptation) rather than clip content.

We ran a three-part validity audit. First, we stratified Top-1 accuracy by clip position 1–5 and found perfectly flat profiles for both DE [4.40%, 4.29%, 4.48%, 4.38%, 4.10%] and PSD [3.84%, 4.39%, 4.29%, 4.13%, 4.31%] — no monotonic trend, no shortcut. Second, we sealed a data leakage bug in the normalization pipeline: for PSD features it caused a 13× reduction in cross-fold variance, confirming the leak was inflating apparent stability. Critically, it did not inflate mean accuracy — so the baseline is valid, just previously miscalibrated. Third, we tried raw 200Hz EEG with temporal self-attention (O(T²) at T=400) and hit OOM, which pointed us toward compressed features.

One positive finding from this phase: within-concept prediction consistency was 24.3% vs. a random baseline of 17.8% (K=10 chunk window). The model is systematically coherent — when it's wrong, it tends to be wrong in the same direction within a concept block. That means the errors aren't random noise; they reflect genuine but confused semantic structure. This hinted there was real signal to find.

Conclusion: DE at 4.37% ± 2.64% Top-1 is a clean, artifact-free baseline for 40-way concept classification. Now we could build on top of it.

Phase 2 — Final Project What If We Replaced Supervised Labels with CLIP?

That baseline used a supervised MLP — it needed concept labels at test time. We asked: what if the model never sees concept labels? Instead, align EEG to frozen CLIP embeddings, then retrieve at test time by cosine similarity to a gallery. That's zero-shot: no labels required, just a CLIP embedding for each concept.

We built NeuroCLIP: a lightweight CNN encoder that maps 62-channel × 5-band Differential Entropy features into a 512-D L2-normalized embedding space, trained with 40-way cross-entropy against a frozen CLIP gallery (image + text average — empirically the best combination). Leave-One-Block-Out 7-fold CV, 21 subjects, within-subject.

Result: 4.60% ± 2.70% Top-1 R@1 — essentially matching our supervised DE baseline (4.37%) while requiring zero concept labels at test time. The zero-shot framing is the contribution: you can swap the CLIP gallery for any concept set without retraining.

Phase 3 — The Discovery Why Do Some Concepts Decode 2× Better Than Others?

Once we had per-concept R@1 scores, the variance was striking — some concepts hit 10–15%, others sat near chance. We had 40 concepts and 21 subjects. The obvious hypothesis: concepts that are more distinct in CLIP space should be easier to decode.

We tested it. Per-concept CLIP isolation score vs. per-concept R@1: r = 0.036, p = 0.83 — completely null. CLIP geometry doesn't predict what the brain can decode. That was genuinely surprising and sent us back to look at the data differently.

We looked at semantic categories instead. SEED-DV has 9 groups: Animals, Nature, Food, Sports, Music, People, Vehicles, Urban, Other. When we split into Activity (Sports + Music + People: 9 concepts) vs. Passive (the remaining 31), the result was stark:

People: 8.6%. Music: 6.5%. Sports: 6.1%. Nature: 3.2%. Food: 3.9%. The top three categories are all activities; the bottom three are all passive. And critically — activity concepts are not more CLIP-isolated. The effect is not explained by CLIP geometry.

Phase 4 — Ruling Out Alternatives Is This Just Louder EEG Signal?

The obvious counter-argument: maybe activity videos just produce stronger EEG responses — higher amplitude — which makes them easier to classify. We went back to the raw DE values and ran t-tests on activity vs. passive concepts for every frequency band (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma). Every single band: p > 0.05, not significant. Raw EEG amplitude does not differ between activity and passive concepts.

The advantage is in the learned multi-channel representational pattern, not signal strength. This points to action observation circuits — the premotor cortex, superior temporal sulcus, and mirror neuron system — producing more consistent, discriminable spatial patterns across subjects when viewing dynamic human action, which NeuroCLIP's contrastive training learns to exploit.

We also checked whether the effect was a one-session fluke. Activity > Passive in 7 out of 7 sessions, mean gap +2.96 percentage points, p=0.0002***. No session × category interaction — the gap doesn't shrink over time.

And NeuroCLIP learned real semantic geometry: category centroids in EEG embedding space correlate with CLIP centroids at ρ = 0.645***. The model isn't encoding noise — it found the semantic structure of the 40-concept space.

Phase 5 — The Payoff A Design Principle That Actually Helps

If CLIP geometry had driven decodability, the right move for a BCI lab would be: pick concepts that are maximally CLIP-isolated. We ran that optimization — predicted gain: +0.27 pp. Essentially nothing.

The right move is: pick activity concepts. Greedy top-10 by R@1 → 6.97% vs. all-40 → 4.50%. That's +55% relative improvement, +2.47 pp. The top-10 are activity-skewed without exception: guitar, boxing, drums, dancing, running, skiing, construction, crowd, piano, computer.

Actionable recommendation: For any EEG-BCI lab designing a stimulus library — prioritize dynamic human action scenes over static object images. The gain comes not from your model architecture or your CLIP gallery, but from how consistently the human brain encodes action concepts across subjects and sessions.

What We Explored (47 Figures, 45 Scripts)

Beyond the core story, we ran extensive supporting analyses to validate every step: confusion matrix structure (within-category errors dominate), CLIP modality ablation (image+text > image-only or text-only), retrieval at R@1/R@5/MRR, frequency band ablation (theta removal hurts most), brain region ablation (parietal and occipital regions most important), subject scaling curves (flat — individual fingerprints dominate, not data quantity), inter-subject consistency, noise ceiling estimation (18.1% of a 58.9% RSA ceiling — large room to grow), and cross-metric Spearman correlation across all per-concept metrics.

Most of these are robustness checks that didn't change the conclusion — but running them meant we could answer every possible "but what if…" question. That's the lab notebook philosophy: explore widely, report tightly.
